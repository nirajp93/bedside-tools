The question

What the starting point is

How likely the diagnosis is in a patient like this one before you examine: the setting, the story, the age. It matters as much as the exam: the same finding lands very differently from 10% and from 60%.

What a likelihood ratio does

It multiplies the odds. As a rough guide between 10% and 90%, ratios of 2, 5 and 10 raise the probability by about 15, 30 and 45 points, and 0.5, 0.2 and 0.1 lower it by the same. A ratio near 1 changes nothing, however classic the finding.

Where the exam helps most

In the middle. At very low or very high starting points even a strong finding rarely moves the probability across a decision line, so examine hardest where you are least sure.

Focused stroke exam

Score the NIH Stroke Scale by what you are doing: look, talk, move, feel. Treatment decisions (thrombolysis, thrombectomy) belong to the stroke team; a Neuro emergencies tool covering them is in progress.

Which territory?
Posterior circulation: easy to miss
  • Dizziness with nystagmus and unsteadiness (acute vestibular syndrome)
    Do HINTS. Any central sign makes stroke likely; see Narrow.
  • Can he walk?
    Truncal ataxia is not scored on the NIHSS. A patient who cannot sit or stand unaided with a near-normal NIHSS may have a cerebellar stroke.
  • A low NIHSS does not exclude stroke
    The scale weights anterior-circulation deficits (language, neglect, limb weakness); posterior strokes can score 0–2 and still be disabling.
