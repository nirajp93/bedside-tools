Consent and time-out

Say it aloud, with everyone stopped

  • Consent: what, why, the common and the serious risks, alternatives, who is doing it. In an emergency with no one able to consent, document why it could not wait.
  • Time-out: right patient (two identifiers), right procedure, right side and site marked, consent signed, allergies (chlorhexidine, lidocaine), anticoagulants and the latest counts, imaging at the bedside, who to call if it goes wrong.
  • Ultrasound before you mark: fluid pocket, vessel patency, the structures to avoid.

Why: wrong-site and wrong-side procedures happen when the team assumes; the time-out is the one moment everyone checks the same facts.

Sterile technique

Central lines: the full bundle, every time

  • Hand hygiene before touching the site or the kit.
  • Skin prep with an alcohol-based chlorhexidine solution, left to dry.
  • Maximal barrier: cap, mask, sterile gown, sterile gloves and a full-body sterile drape.
  • An insertion checklist kept by someone other than the operator, who is empowered to stop the procedure.
  • An all-in-one kit or cart; ultrasound guidance.

Arterial lines: cap, mask, sterile gloves and a small fenestrated drape at least; the full barrier for femoral or axillary sites.

Why: most early line infections start from skin organisms carried in at insertion; the bundle removes those routes together.

More

SHEA/IDSA/APIC 2022 rates chlorhexidine-alcohol skin prep and ultrasound guidance as high-quality evidence, and hand hygiene, maximal barrier, the checklist with an empowered observer, and the all-in-one kit as moderate. Arterial line barrier: CDC/HICPAC 2011 (IB; maximal barrier for femoral or axillary, II).

When a central line, and when not

Needed for what the veins of the arm cannot take

  • Vasopressors beyond a short peripheral start; vesicant or irritant infusions; many incompatible infusions.
  • Dialysis, CRRT or plasma exchange (a dialysis catheter).
  • No usable peripheral access; central venous pressure or saturation; transvenous pacing.

Pick another site when the skin over it is infected or burned, the vein is clotted or absent on ultrasound, or a device or surgery is in the way.

Where the tip should sit

Jugular and subclavian: lower superior vena cava, outside the heart

  • Right-sided lines: a tip at or a little above the carina on the film lies outside the pericardium.
  • Left-sided lines: the catheter crosses to reach the superior vena cava; a tip below the carina is more likely to lie along the vein rather than against its wall.
  • Confirm the tip and look for a pneumothorax (film or ultrasound) before using a jugular or subclavian line; a femoral line needs no film for its tip.

Why: the carina sits above the top of the pericardium, so it marks where a tip can no longer cause tamponade if it erodes through.

More

Carina as a landmark: Stonelake and Bodenham, Br J Anaesth 2006. Chest film before first use of a jugular or subclavian dialysis catheter: KDIGO 2012 5.4.4 (1B); for other central lines, confirming the tip as soon as practical is ASA 2020 practice.

Daily care and removal

Every day: is it still needed?

  • Remove lines that are no longer essential; ask on rounds every day.
  • Chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing; transparent dressing changed at least every 7 days, gauze every 2 days, either at once if damp, loose or soiled.
  • Daily chlorhexidine bathing for ICU patients.
  • Do not replace lines on a schedule, or over a guidewire, to prevent infection.
  • Removal: flat or head down, hub occluded; remove while the chest pressure is positive (breath held or humming; on the ventilator, during a delivered breath); press, then an occlusive dressing.

When and where

Beat-to-beat pressure and frequent gases; radial first

  • When: shock or vasopressors, titrated infusions for blood pressure, frequent arterial gases.
  • Site: radial first; femoral or axillary when radial fails. The brachial artery is an end artery, so many avoid it.
  • Allen test: poor accuracy; not a reliable predictor of hand ischemia.
  • Ultrasound raises first-attempt success.
  • Never inject drugs into an arterial line; label it.

Why: the radial artery has a collateral supply through the hand and is easy to compress, so a complication there costs least.

More

Allen test, site trade-offs: Saugel 2020 review. Ultrasound for radial cannulation: 12 trials, 2,432 adults, first-attempt success RR 1.35 (Moussa Pacha 2018). Avoiding the brachial site is common practice, not a guideline rule; CDC 2011 lists radial, brachial or dorsalis pedis as preferred over femoral or axillary for infection.

Zero and level

Zero to air; level the transducer with the heart

Open the transducer to air and zero it. Then keep it at the phlebostatic axis: fourth intercostal space, mid-axillary line, the level of the right atrium. Re-level whenever the bed or patient moves.

Problems

Hand, bleeding, infection

  • Pale, cold or painful hand, or slow refill beyond the site: remove the line.
  • Bleeding or disconnection: luer-lock connections, the site in view, pressure.
  • Infection: same bundle thinking as a central line; the femoral site carries more.
  • No trace: check the stopcock, flush, then the patient (pulse) before trusting the number.

Thoracentesis

Ultrasound first; above the rib; stop at symptoms or 1.5 L

Ultrasound for every pleural fluid procedureMark the site with ultrasound, at the bedside, just before the needle (except in an emergency).
  • Site: the deepest safe pocket, usually posterolateral; pass just above a rib, because the vessels and nerve run under it. Stay more than about 6 cm lateral to the spine, where the intercostal artery runs exposed.
  • How much: drain at most 1.5 L at one attempt.
  • Stop for chest tightness, pain, persistent cough or worsening breathlessness.
  • Film after: not needed if it was straightforward and they feel well; image if symptoms, air was drawn back, or several passes were needed.

Why: chest tightness or cough during drainage can mean pleural pressure is falling steeply because the lung cannot follow; BTS uses these symptoms as the stop signal, though they predict re-expansion edema imperfectly.

More

Clotting: the BTS 2023 statement does not ask for a clotting screen when there is no history of coagulopathy, and in the largest series bleeding was not associated with INR, PTT or platelets. For elective warfarin interruption it asks for an INR of 1.5 or less; SIR 2019 suggests 2.0 to 3.0 or less for low-risk procedures (Before tab). Pleural manometry did not reduce chest discomfort compared with symptom-guided drainage (Learn, trials).

Re-expansion edema is rare: clinical in 0.5% and on the film in 2.2% of 185 taps of 1 L or more (Feller-Kopman 2007); about 1 in 1,000 of 9,320 taps (Ault 2015). Whether volume predicts it differs between studies.

BTS 2023: ultrasound mandatory; 1.5 L; stop rulesHelm 2013: 6 cm

A large effusion through a drain

Drain at a written rate; clamp for cough or pain

  • Write the rate in the orders (BTS gives 500 mL an hour as an example).
  • Clamp at once for repeated coughing or chest pain, whatever the volume so far.
  • No suction soon after insertion.
  • Do not clamp a bubbling drain unless a pleural specialist is supervising.

Why: uncontrolled drainage lets the lung re-expand too fast, and a clamped tube with an air leak can become a tension pneumothorax.

BTS 2023 clinical statement

When to call now

Senior, surgeon or pleural team

  • New hypotension, rising oxygen need, or distress after a pleural procedure.
  • Spreading subcutaneous emphysema, or a large air leak that does not settle.
  • Fresh blood draining fast, or a falling hemoglobin.
  • The drain has fallen out or moved, or the dressing is soaked with fluid.

Paracentesis

Left lower quadrant, lateral to the rectus, on ultrasound

  • Site: about two fingerbreadths (3 cm) above and two fingerbreadths medial to the anterior superior iliac spine. The wall there is thinner and the pool deeper than in the midline.
  • Stay lateral to the rectus sheath: the inferior epigastric artery runs up along it. Avoid visible veins (collaterals) and scars.
  • Ultrasound to find the pocket and look for vessels in the path.
  • Z-track (pull the skin down before the needle goes in) to reduce leaking afterwards.
  • Clotting: do not routinely give plasma or platelets; a raised INR or low platelets is not a contraindication. Exception: clinically evident hyperfibrinolysis or DIC, where Runyon 2009 advises against tapping until it is treated; discuss with a senior.

Why: a lateral site checked on ultrasound keeps the needle away from the inferior epigastric artery and wall collaterals; in cirrhosis the INR does not predict procedural bleeding.

Problems after a tap

Leak, bleeding, kidneys and sodium

  • Persistent leak: a stoma bag over the site; a Z-track next time.
  • Bleeding: abdominal wall hematoma or, rarely, blood in the abdomen; hypotension or a falling hemoglobin in the hours after needs imaging and a senior.
  • Circulatory dysfunction days later: rising creatinine, falling sodium, faster reaccumulation. Albumin with large taps roughly halves it compared with other volume expanders (Ginès 1996).
  • Bowel puncture: rare; suspect with feculent fluid or many organisms.
