Optimize the ventilator and sedation first. Not routinely beyond 48 hours. A fixed dose without twitch monitoring, or titration to train-of-four, are both acceptable. Paralysis while prone is optional.

More

Screen every patient regularly with the CAM-ICU or the ICDSC (good practice statement). The ICDSC is an eight-item checklist scored over a shift; 4 or more is delirium.

Most ICU delirium is hypoactive: quiet, drowsy, inattentive. It is missed unless you test attention.

Treating delirium

Fix causes and use the non-drug bundle. No routine antipsychotic.

Reorient · glasses and hearing aids · daylight, dark quiet nights · mobilize · lighten sedation

Why: in two large placebo-controlled trials haloperidol did not improve the primary outcome (delirium-free days in one, days alive and out of hospital in the other), so drugs are for distress or dangerous agitation, not for the diagnosis.

Agitation that is blocking weaning or extubation: dexmedetomidine. Check the QTc before and during any antipsychotic. QT risk checker
More

What the guidelines say. 2018: suggest not routinely using haloperidol or an atypical antipsychotic. 2025: unable to recommend for or against antipsychotics. No drug is recommended just to prevent delirium (2018). Melatonin is suggested over no melatonin (2025); the guideline gives no dose.

What the trials used (not a recommendation). Haloperidol 2.5 mg IV three times daily plus 2.5 mg as needed, at most 20 mg a day (AID-ICU). Quetiapine 50 mg every 12 hours, raised by 50 mg per dose each day to at most 200 mg every 12 hours (a 36-patient pilot).

Haloperidol's label. Intramuscular only, 2 to 5 mg, at most 20 mg a day. It is not approved for intravenous use; if given IV, monitor the ECG for QTc prolongation and arrhythmias.

Stop any antipsychotic before the patient leaves the ICU unless there is a reason to continue.

Pain drives agitation

A patient who cannot speak shows pain as restlessness and fighting the ventilator. Score it, treat it, then decide whether any sedative is needed. Tap each row to score.

Light beats deep

Light sedation means the patient wakes to voice. It shortens time on the ventilator. Tap a level to see what it looks like at the bedside.

Why not a benzodiazepine infusion

Midazolam and lorazepam accumulate, wake slowly and are linked to more delirium. They keep three jobs: alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, seizures, and deep sedation when propofol cannot be used.

Propofol or dexmedetomidine

Propofol can take a patient to any depth and stops breathing as it goes. Dexmedetomidine gives a rousable, sleep-like state and has little effect on the drive to breathe, but it is unreliable for deep sedation and it slows the heart.

Delirium is inattention

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating failure of attention. The agitated form is obvious. The quiet form is far more common and is found only by testing. Each day of it is linked to worse outcomes, and the things that cause it are mostly things we do.

What antipsychotics do and do not do

They calm. They have not been shown to shorten delirium or coma. That is why they are kept for distressing symptoms or dangerous agitation, at a low dose, with a stop date.

Paralysis hides everything

A paralysed patient cannot move, grimace or breathe against you, so pain and awareness are invisible. Sedate deeply first. When the dose is titrated, the nerve stimulator shows how much block there is.
