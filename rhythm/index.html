AV block on the strip

  • First degree: PR longer than 200 ms, every P conducted.
  • Mobitz I (Wenckebach): PR lengthens beat by beat until a P is not conducted.
  • Mobitz II: PR constant, then a P is not conducted.
  • High grade: two or more consecutive P waves not conducted.
  • Third degree: no P is conducted; atria and ventricles beat independently.

Why it matters: block in the AV node (Mobitz I, narrow escape) is usually benign and responds to atropine. Block below the node (Mobitz II, wide escape) can progress to asystole without warning.

More: where is a 2:1 block?

With 2:1 conduction there are never two conducted beats in a row, so the PR cannot be seen to lengthen and the strip alone cannot separate Mobitz I from II.

  • In the node: narrow QRS, long PR on conducted beats, improves with atropine or exercise.
  • Below the node: wide QRS, worsens with atropine or exercise, improves with carotid massage.

Why: a pacemaker treats symptoms from a slow rate, or prevents asystole where the block is below the AV node. Where neither applies it adds risk without benefit.

More: reversible causes, and which lead

Look for a reversible cause first: inferior MI; beta blocker, diltiazem or verapamil, digoxin, amiodarone, clonidine, donepezil, ivabradine; hyperkalemia; hypothyroidism; Lyme carditis (treat the infection; a pacemaker is usually not needed); sleep apnea; raised intracranial pressure; hypothermia. Bradycardia that is mainly nocturnal should prompt a sleep apnea screen.

Which lead: with an EF of 36 to 50% and substantial ventricular pacing expected, physiologic pacing (biventricular or conduction-system pacing) is reasonable to avoid pacing-induced cardiomyopathy Class 2a, HRS 2023. With EF above 35% and little ventricular pacing expected, a conventional right ventricular lead with pacing minimized is reasonable Class 2a, HRS 2023.

Bundle branch and fascicular blocks

  • RBBB: QRS ≥ 120 ms, rsR′ in V1 to V2, wide S in I and V6.
  • LBBB: QRS ≥ 120 ms, broad notched R in I, aVL, V5 to V6, no septal q in those leads.
  • Left anterior fascicular block: axis −45° to −90°, qR in aVL, rS in the inferior leads, QRS under 120 ms.
  • Left posterior fascicular block: axis +90° to +180°, rS in I and aVL, qR in III and aVF.
  • Bifascicular block: RBBB with either fascicular block. The 2018 guideline still uses the term; the 2009 ECG statement discourages both it and "trifascicular block".
New LBBB: get an echocardiogram to look for structural heart disease Class 1.

Criteria: AHA/ACCF/HRS ECG standardization, 2009. LBBB and ischemia: see EKG emergencies for the Sgarbossa rules.

Pre-excitation (WPW pattern)

PR under 120 ms, a slurred upstroke (delta wave), and a widened QRS in sinus rhythm.

  • Orthodromic AVRT: down the node, up the pathway. Narrow and regular. Treat as SVT; adenosine is fine.
  • Antidromic AVRT: down the pathway. Wide and regular; looks like VT.
  • Pre-excited AF: wide, irregular, very fast, with QRS shape changing beat to beat.
Pre-excited AF: no digoxin, IV amiodarone, beta blocker, diltiazem or verapamil Class 3: Harm. Give IV procainamide or ibutilide Class 1, or cardiovert.

Why: with the AV node blocked, conduction runs over the accessory pathway, which has no built-in delay.

More: the asymptomatic delta wave

Abrupt loss of pre-excitation during an exercise test marks a pathway that cannot conduct fast, and is low risk Class 1. An EP study to stratify risk is reasonable Class 2a. At EP study, a shortest pre-excited R–R interval of 250 ms or less in AF marks high risk. Symptomatic patients are referred for ablation Class 1.

QT interval

  • Measure in lead II or V5 to V6, whichever is longest, to the point where the tangent of the T-wave downslope meets the baseline.
  • Prolonged: QTc of 450 ms or more in men, 460 ms or more in women.
  • Torsades risk rises with QTc above 500 ms, or a rise of more than 60 ms from baseline.
  • Bazett's formula overcorrects at fast heart rates; Fridericia's is the usual bedside alternative.

Why: a long QT means ventricular cells are slow to repolarize. An early afterdepolarization landing in that window, typically after a pause, starts torsades.

More: congenital long QT
  • LQT1: events with exertion, especially swimming.
  • LQT2: sudden noise, and the postpartum period.
  • LQT3: events at rest or in sleep.

Beta blocker for long QT syndrome with a resting QTc above 470 ms Class 1, 2017 guideline. Avoid QT-prolonging drugs and keep potassium and magnesium replete. Modern review: Schwartz and Crotti, N Engl J Med 2025.

AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009; AHA/ACCF torsades statement 2010

Channelopathies and inherited patterns

  • Brugada, type 1: coved ST elevation of 2 mm or more in V1 or V2 (standard or higher intercostal position). Treat fever promptly and avoid sodium-channel-blocking drugs. With a spontaneous type 1 pattern: ICD after cardiac arrest, sustained ventricular arrhythmia, or recent syncope presumed arrhythmic Class 1. Quinidine for recurrent ICD shocks Class 1; isoproterenol is used for electrical storm 2013 consensus.
  • CPVT: bidirectional or polymorphic VT with exercise or emotion, normal resting ECG. Beta blocker; flecainide is added if events continue.
  • Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy: epsilon wave and T inversion in V1 to V3; VT with left bundle shape.
  • Short QT and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patterns: refer.

2017 AHA/ACC/HRS ventricular arrhythmia guideline; 2013 HRS/EHRA/APHRS consensus on inherited arrhythmia syndromes.

Electrolytes and drugs on the ECG

  • Hyperkalemia: peaked T, then long PR and flat P, then wide QRS, then sine wave. It can imitate any block or wide rhythm.
  • Hypokalemia: flat T, prominent U wave, long QU interval.
  • Hypocalcemia: long ST segment (long QT with a normal T). Hypercalcemia: short QT.
  • Digoxin effect: scooped ST depression; not toxicity. Digoxin toxicity: atrial tachycardia with block, bidirectional VT, regularized AF.
  • Tricyclic or other sodium-channel poisoning: QRS over 100 ms predicts seizures and over 160 ms ventricular arrhythmia; tall terminal R in aVR. Treat with sodium bicarbonate 1 to 2 mEq/kg IV.

Electrolyte patterns: teaching convention. Tricyclic thresholds: Boehnert and Lovejoy 1985; AHA 2023 toxicology update. Repletion doses: Electrolyte repletion.

Pacemakers and ICDs on the ward

Reading the mode: first letter is the chamber paced, second the chamber sensed, third the response to sensing (I inhibit, D both inhibit and trigger, O none), fourth R for rate response. VVI paces and senses the ventricle only; DDD does both chambers; VOO and DOO pace at a fixed rate and ignore the heart's own beats.

  • Failure to capture: spike with no complex after it. Check potassium, ischemia, new drugs, and lead position on a chest film.
  • Undersensing: spikes land where they should not, on top of native beats.
  • Oversensing: unexpected pauses; the device is inhibited by something that is not a heartbeat.
  • Pacemaker-mediated tachycardia: paced tachycardia at the upper rate limit in a dual-chamber device.

Magnet: on a pacemaker, asynchronous pacing at the manufacturer's magnet rate. On an ICD, shock therapy is suspended and pacing is unchanged. HRS/ASA 2011 consensus

More: the patient whose ICD fired

One shock and feeling well: arrange device interrogation soon. Three or more shocks in 24 hours, or a shock with syncope or chest pain: emergency evaluation and treat as electrical storm. Expert practice

More

A wearable defibrillator is not routine after MI: in VEST it did not significantly reduce the primary endpoint. When biventricular pacing cannot be achieved, conduction-system pacing is a reasonable alternative Class 2a, HRS 2023.

  • Flecainide, propafenone: not with prior MI, coronary disease or structural heart disease Class 3: Harm. Pair with an AV-nodal blocker.
  • Amiodarone: baseline TSH, liver tests, chest film, pulmonary function with DLCO, and eye exam; TSH and liver tests every 6 months; chest film yearly. It raises warfarin and digoxin effect (reduce both). Simvastatin maximum 20 mg daily.
  • Sotalol: start in hospital with QTc monitoring; dose by kidney function. Do not start with QTc above 450 ms; reduce or stop at 500 ms or more.
  • Dofetilide: start in hospital for at least 3 days. CrCl > 60: 500 mcg BID · 40–60: 250 mcg BID · 20 to < 40: 125 mcg BID · < 20: contraindicated. Do not start if QTc is above 440 ms. ECG 2 to 3 hours after each dose: after the first dose, halve the dose if QTc rises more than 15% or exceeds 500 ms; after any later dose, stop if QTc exceeds 500 ms.
  • Dronedarone: not in NYHA III–IV or recently decompensated heart failure, and not in permanent AF.
  • Ibutilide: 1 mg IV over 10 min (0.01 mg/kg if under 60 kg); may repeat once. Monitor at least 4 hours for torsades.
  • Lidocaine: 1–1.5 mg/kg IV; repeat 0.5–0.75 mg/kg to a maximum of 3 mg/kg; then 1–4 mg/min. Earlier AHA life-support editions

Why class Ic is barred in structural disease: in CAST, flecainide and encainide given after MI suppressed ectopy and raised mortality.

More: choosing a rhythm drug, and newer options

By substrate: no structural heart disease: flecainide, propafenone, dofetilide, dronedarone or sotalol. Coronary disease: no class Ic. Heart failure with reduced EF: amiodarone or dofetilide. 2023 AF guideline

Dofetilide must not be combined with verapamil, hydrochlorothiazide, trimethoprim, cimetidine, ketoconazole, dolutegravir, prochlorperazine or megestrol.

Pill in the pocket: flecainide 200 to 300 mg or propafenone 450 to 600 mg once, after a first monitored dose.

Etripamil nasal spray was FDA-approved in December 2025 for adults to convert acute symptomatic PSVT themselves outside hospital. In the RAPID trial 64% converted by 30 minutes against 31% on placebo. It belongs in discharge and clinic planning for recurrent PSVT; dose and contraindications are in the label.

Adjust to your institution: the inpatient loading protocol for dofetilide and sotalol, and the telemetry QTc policy.
