Oxygen, high flow and NIV

The right saturation, the right device, high flow or NIV for the cause, and the signs that mean intubate now.

High risk: over 65, heart or lung disease, obesity, hypercapnia on the breathing trial, prolonged ventilation, weak cough or airway problems.

Setting up high flow

  • Start at 50 L/min, 37 °C.
  • FiO2 high enough to reach the target, then titrate down.
  • Wean the FiO2 to 0.40 first, then the flow.
  • Back to standard oxygen once FiO2 is 0.40 or less and flow 30 L/min or less.

From the awake-prone meta-trial protocol (BMJ Open 2020), expert practice. FLORALI used 50 L/min with FiO2 1.0 at the start.

High flow or NIV, by cause

Before NIV: contraindications

Do not use NIV

  • Respiratory or cardiac arrest, or about to arrest
  • Cannot protect the airway: coma, seizures (hypoxemic failure; in hypercapnia, NIV in ICU with intubation ready)
  • Copious secretions the patient cannot clear (hypoxemic failure; in hypercapnia, NIV in ICU with intubation ready)
  • Shock not responding to fluids or vasopressors (hypoxemic failure; in hypercapnia, NIV in ICU with intubation ready)
  • No mask will fit: facial trauma, burns, deformity, fixed upper airway obstruction

Use with caution

  • Vomiting or upper GI bleeding
  • Undrained pneumothorax
  • Agitated, cannot cooperate

Arrest: BTS/ICS 2016 R33 (D). No interface: R34 (D). Airway, secretions and shock: the major intubation criteria of Duan 2022 (hypoxemic patients); in hypercapnic coma, NIV in ICU with intubation ready is an option (BTS/ICS). Drain a pneumothorax before NIV (BTS/ICS); the rest of the caution list is expert practice. BTS/ICS: few contraindications are absolute; adverse features raise the risk of failure and should prompt high-dependency or ICU care (R3, C).

Failing: when to intubate

Any one: intubate now

  • Arrest or peri-arrest
  • Cannot protect the airway, or copious secretions (hypoxemic failure; in hypercapnia, NIV in ICU with intubation ready)
  • Shock despite fluids and vasopressors (hypoxemic failure; in hypercapnia, NIV in ICU with intubation ready)
  • Hypoxemic failure: P/F under 100 on NIV or high flow
  • Hypercapnic failure: acidosis persisting or worsening, or pH and rate not improving, despite optimised NIV

Hypoxemic failure: three or more

  • P/F under 150
  • Rate over 35
  • Respiratory muscle fatigue not improving
  • pH under 7.35

Hypoxemic criteria from Duan 2022 (a study protocol in hypoxemic patients; not for hypercapnic patients on NIV). Hypercapnic criteria from BTS/ICS 2016 (R30, R32 A; R33 D); in hypercapnia, drowsiness and severe acidosis call for NIV in ICU with intubation ready, not automatic intubation. On high flow, also the ROX index. Recheck 1–2 hours after any start (expert practice); in hypercapnia, better pH and rate within 4 hours predict success.

The oxygen curve has a cliff

Saturation stays high until the PaO2 nears 60, then falls fast. Targets sit just above the cliff edge. Tap a saturation.

Why oxygen fixes some low saturations and not others

Mismatch responds to oxygen, shunt barely does, and hypoventilation responds while CO2 keeps climbing.

What each device really delivers

A low-flow device gives less than the patient breathes in, so room air dilutes it, more so the faster they breathe.

How high flow works

Enough flow to match the breath, washout of the upper airway, and a little pressure.

CPAP holds the lungs open; bilevel also breathes with them

One pressure all the way through, or a higher pressure on the way in. Tap to compare.

The ROX index

ROX = (SpO2 ÷ FiO2) ÷ respiratory rate, with FiO2 as a fraction. Validated in pneumonia on high flow. Move the slider.

Why waiting can hurt

Non-invasive support buys time; it does not treat the cause. Patients who fail and are intubated late have done worse in observational studies, so each start comes with a time to look again and a line that means stop.

  • High flow failing, intubated at 48 h or later: ICU mortality 66.7% against 39.2% when intubated earlier (Kang 2015, one centre, retrospective).
  • De novo pneumonia on NIV: each extra hour before intubation went with lower survival (Carrillo 2012).
  • NIV failures at high risk (HACOR over 5 at 1 h): hospital mortality 66% when intubated within 12 h against 79% later (Duan 2017).
