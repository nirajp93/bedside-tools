Airway and RSI

Who needs a tube, how hard it will be, how to prepare, which drugs at which dose, and the first five minutes after.

You prepare, assist and manage the physiology. The most experienced airway operator available holds the laryngoscope; never plan to intubate alone.

Who needs a tube now

Failing to oxygenate or ventilate, cannot protect the airway, or an airway that will close

  • Failing despite high flow or NIV: the oxygen tool has the failure signs and when to stop waiting.
  • Cannot protect the airway: pooled secretions or vomit with no effective cough, or consciousness too low to keep it clear.
  • The expected course: swelling that is progressing (angioedema, airway burns, neck hematoma), or a procedure or transfer that cannot be done safely otherwise.

Why: intubating early, with time to prepare and resuscitate, is safer than intubating late in a crash.

Resuscitate before you intubate

Blood pressure support ready before induction, not after

  • A vasopressor running, or push-dose ready, when blood pressure is low or the shock index is high.
  • Fluid only if they are likely to respond to it: a routine 500 mL bolus for everyone did not prevent collapse (PREPARE II).
  • Two working IV lines; an arterial line if time allows and they are unstable.

Why: induction drugs remove sympathetic tone and positive pressure lowers venous return at the same moment; a patient already relying on both falls fastest.

The first five minutes

Prove the tube is in, then sedation, settings and blood pressure

Waveform capnographyA sustained CO2 waveform confirms the tube. No recognisable trace means failed intubation until proven otherwise, even with a good view and chest rise.
  1. Confirm: waveform CO2 on every breath; listen both sides and over the stomach.
  2. Secure: note the depth at the teeth; chest film for position.
  3. Sedation and pain relief started now: rocuronium paralysis lasts far longer than the induction drug, and a paralysed patient cannot show you they are awake.
  4. Ventilator: initial settings from predicted body weight (in the ventilator tool); match the minute ventilation they were managing in severe acidosis.
  5. Blood pressure checked frequently for the first half hour; treat a fall early.

Why: esophageal intubation, awareness under paralysis and hypotension all happen in these minutes, and each is easy to prevent if looked for.

Desaturation after the tube

Displaced, obstructed, pneumothorax, equipment

  • Displaced: CO2 trace lost or changed, depth moved, one side silent (mainstem).
  • Obstructed: secretions, kinked or bitten tube, high peak pressure: pass a suction catheter.
  • Pneumothorax: one side silent with low blood pressure, no lung sliding.
  • Equipment: disconnect and bag with 100% oxygen to take the ventilator out of the question.

Why: disconnecting and hand-bagging separates a patient problem from a machine problem in one step.
