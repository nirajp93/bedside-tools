Salt-retaining (mineralocorticoid) effect from Liu 2013: fludrocortisone 0.1 mg has about the salt-retaining effect of hydrocortisone 20 mg or prednisone 50 mg. At equal anti-inflammatory doses, prednisone retains far less salt than hydrocortisone; methylprednisolone, dexamethasone and betamethasone hardly any.

Deflazacort 7.5 mg is also listed as equal to hydrocortisone 20 mg (ESE Table 1).

Who is at risk

Physiologic dose and suppression

  • Physiologic daily dose: hydrocortisone 15–25 mg, prednisone or prednisolone 4–6 mg, methylprednisolone 3–5 mg, dexamethasone 0.25–0.5 mg. The adrenal makes about 9–10 mg of cortisol a day.
  • At risk: more than physiologic for 3–4 weeks or longer, by any route. Assume suppression with Cushingoid features.
  • Under 3–4 weeks at any dose: stop without a taper and without testing.
  • Current or recent long-term use, not tested: stress-dose when ill or having a procedure. After a short course this is not routinely needed, but treat hypotension as adrenal crisis.
  • Do not test while the dose is still above physiologic or still needed for the disease.

Why: Steroid above the body's own output suppresses CRH and ACTH; after weeks the adrenal cortex atrophies, and recovery can take months to years.

More

The guideline treats exposure as several risks together: potency (dexamethasone, betamethasone and fluticasone highest), route (systemic and joint injections highest), dose, duration (over 3 months highest), obesity and older age. The tool counts 3 weeks as long-term (the cautious end of "3–4 weeks") and uses 5 mg prednisone-equivalent as the exposure cut-off.

Older teaching put the line at 20 mg of prednisone for 3 weeks; the 2024 guideline does not use it.

Repeated short courses: UK Society for Endocrinology guidance also counts repeated courses as a risk: 3 or more courses in 12 months of prednisolone 40 mg a day or more for a week or longer.

No fludrocortisone in steroid-induced adrenal insufficiency: aldosterone is controlled by renin and potassium, so it is preserved.

Inhaled, topical, joint injections

Non-oral steroids can suppress too

  • Think of adrenal insufficiency with: symptoms on current or recent non-oral steroid; several formulations at once; high-dose inhaled or topical; inhaled or topical for over a year; a joint injection in the past 2 months; a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor (ritonavir, cobicistat, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, clarithromycin).
  • Biochemical suppression in pooled studies: inhaled 7.8% overall (27% if over a year), joint injections 52%, topical 4.7%, nasal 4.2%.
  • Fluticasone is the inhaled steroid most often linked to adrenal insufficiency and Cushing syndrome.

Why: Enough drug is absorbed from lungs, skin and joints to reach the pituitary, and CYP3A4 inhibitors stop it being cleared.

More

Joint injections often suppress cortisol for about 4 weeks; recovery can be confirmed in the following 4 weeks. Most of these figures are biochemical; symptoms are much rarer.

Adrenal crisisHydrocortisone 100 mg IV or IM now, rapid IV saline, then 200 mg over 24 h until recovered.

Why: A suppressed adrenal cannot raise cortisol under stress, and vomiting or fasting stops tablets being absorbed.

More

The guideline bases these doses on practice and earlier UK and Endocrine Society guidance, and says lower doses are also routinely used for moderate and major stress. US practice often uses less: for rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis or lupus with elective hip or knee replacement, the 2022 ACR/AAHKS guideline suggests the usual daily dose instead of extra steroid (conditional, low quality). Two small trials found no more hypotension with the usual dose (Learn, Trials).

Oral for minor stress without instability or prolonged vomiting or diarrhea; parenteral for moderate or major stress, procedures under general or regional anesthesia, or when oral intake is not possible. High body weight may need more.

Adrenal crisis

Treat first, test later

  • Suspect it with hypotension or shock, collapse, or acute abdominal symptoms, vomiting or diarrhea in anyone on, or recently off, any steroid, by any route, at any dose.
  • Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV or IM immediately; rapid IV 0.9% saline; then 200 mg over 24 h (infusion, or 50 mg every 6 h) until recovered. Treat the trigger.
  • Do not wait for labs or imaging. Giving it to someone who turns out not to need it is safe.
  • Labs may show low sodium (with raised urine sodium), high potassium, low glucose, raised urea and creatinine, lymphocytes or eosinophils up; none is required for the diagnosis.

Why: Adrenal crisis is volume loss and vasodilation; without cortisol, fluids and pressors often fail.

More

Shock is typically out of proportion to the trigger. Triggers include infection, surgery, trauma, missed doses and gastroenteritis. Risk is higher with past crises, age over 65 and more illness, and at the time of tapering or stopping. High potassium is less typical than in primary adrenal failure because aldosterone is preserved. In very high body weight, higher doses may be considered. Refer to endocrinology after any crisis.

5 mg1 mg (or hydrocortisone 20 mg, down 5 mg)4 weeks, if no symptoms or testing is reassuring
  • Taper only when the disease is controlled and the steroid is no longer needed.
  • Switch dexamethasone or betamethasone to prednisone or hydrocortisone once the long-acting drug is no longer needed; avoid evening doses.
  • At a physiologic dose: keep tapering and watch for symptoms, or check a morning cortisol.
  • Morning cortisol at 8–9 am, at least 24 h after the last dose: over 10 µg/dL (300 nmol/L) recovered, can stop; 5–10 continue the physiologic dose and repeat in weeks to months; under 5 (150 nmol/L) continue and repeat in a few months.
  • Withdrawal symptoms: go back to the last tolerated dose and slow down.
  • Endocrinology: no recovery after a year on a physiologic dose, or any adrenal crisis.

Why: Above physiologic doses the risk is flare and withdrawal, not adrenal insufficiency, so steps can be larger; near 5 mg the axis has to restart, so steps get smaller.

More

Cortisol is a continuum, not a cut-off, and assays differ: know your laboratory's values. Falsely high with oral estrogen or pregnancy; falsely low with low albumin or binding globulin (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, long critical illness), night shifts, jet lag. The guideline excludes dexamethasone from the 24-hour hold; dexamethasone is not measured by most cortisol assays but keeps suppressing the axis, so switch off it before testing (interpretation).

No routine dynamic test. If cortisol stays between 5 and 10, a 250 µg ACTH test can be considered: a peak under about 18 µg/dL (500 nmol/L) at 30 or 60 minutes suggests adrenal insufficiency, depending on assay. The 1 µg test is not advised.

Withdrawal syndrome (fatigue, aches, sleep and mood change) usually appears once prednisone is under 15 mg; adrenal insufficiency is a concern once the taper reaches 5–7.5 mg, or earlier with critical illness or vomiting.

Withdrawal, adrenal insufficiency, or flare?

Why: The three overlap in symptoms; the dose, blood pressure, sodium and glucose, and disease markers separate them.

More

Check first: blood pressure lying and standing, weight, sodium, potassium and glucose, and the disease's own markers (for example ESR or CRP). Only once on a physiologic dose: morning cortisol, 24 h after the last dose.

Faded means less likely, never excluded. Withdrawal and adrenal insufficiency often coexist; Cushingoid features mean the axis is still assumed to be suppressed.
