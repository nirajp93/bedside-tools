DOAC dose for this patient

Why the doses differ

Each DOAC is cleared by the kidney to a different degree: dabigatran about 80%, edoxaban about 50%, rivaroxaban about a third, apixaban about a quarter. The more renal the clearance, the earlier the dose falls as creatinine clearance drops.

Apixaban is the exception in form: its reduction needs two of three criteria (age ≥ 80, weight ≤ 60 kg, creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dL), because that is how the ARISTOTLE trial dosed it. One criterion alone keeps the full dose. Under-dosing outside these rules gives up stroke protection without a proven bleeding benefit (Class 3: Harm for non-evidence-based doses).

Edoxaban is not used when creatinine clearance is above 95 mL/min: the drug is cleared so quickly that ischemic stroke was more frequent than with warfarin in that subgroup of ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48.

Creatinine clearance here is Cockcroft-Gault with actual body weight (the rivaroxaban label specifies actual weight). It is not the eGFR on the lab report.

Bleeding risk · HAS-BLED (optional)

Why a high score is not a reason to withhold

The same things that raise bleeding risk (age, prior stroke, hypertension, kidney disease) also raise stroke risk, so the patients with high HAS-BLED scores are usually the ones who gain the most from anticoagulation. The guideline says bleeding scores should not be used in isolation to decide eligibility (Class 3: No Benefit). Use the score as a checklist of what to fix: blood pressure, NSAIDs, alcohol, an unneeded antiplatelet.

AF with rapid ventricular response

Why the ejection fraction changes the drug

Diltiazem and verapamil slow the AV node but also weaken contraction. A ventricle that is already failing cannot afford the negative inotropy, and in trials of diltiazem after MI, patients with EF below 40% developed more heart failure. Beta blockers are also negative inotropes acutely, so start low in decompensated failure. Digoxin and amiodarone slow the rate without that cost, which is why they are the fallbacks.

In pre-excited AF the impulses reach the ventricle through an accessory pathway. Blocking the AV node removes the competing route and can speed conduction down the pathway, turning AF into ventricular fibrillation.

Resting rate target without heart failure: guided by symptoms, in general below 100 to 110 bpm (Class 2a; RACE II).

Cardioversion: is it safe for stroke risk?

Why three weeks before and four weeks after

Thrombus forms in the left atrial appendage when the atrium stops contracting. Three weeks of anticoagulation lets an existing clot organise or resolve; imaging (transesophageal echo) is the shortcut that looks for it directly.

After conversion the ECG shows sinus rhythm, but the atrium stays mechanically stunned for days to weeks. New clot can form during that window and embolise when contraction returns, which is why anticoagulation continues at least four weeks whatever the baseline score.

    Rate or rhythm control

    • AF diagnosed within the past year: rhythm control can be useful to reduce hospitalisation, stroke and death. Class 2a · EAST-AFNET 4
    • New heart failure with reduced EF and AF: suspect arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy; early, aggressive rhythm control. Class 1
    • HFrEF on guideline-directed therapy, suitable candidate: catheter ablation. Class 1
    • Younger, few comorbidities, symptomatic paroxysmal AF: catheter ablation as first-line therapy. Class 1
    • Symptomatic AF: rhythm control can be useful to improve symptoms. Class 2a
    Why early rhythm control

    AF remodels the atrium: the longer it persists, the more fibrosis and electrical change, and the harder sinus rhythm is to restore ("AF begets AF"). EAST-AFNET 4 randomised patients within a year of diagnosis and found a lower rate of a composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or hospitalisation for heart failure or acute coronary syndrome with a rhythm-control strategy. Older trials that showed no advantage enrolled patients with long-standing AF and used antiarrhythmic drugs without ablation.

    Left atrial appendage occlusion

    • CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥ 2 and a contraindication to long-term anticoagulation from a nonreversible cause: percutaneous occlusion is reasonable. Class 2a
    • High risk of major bleeding on anticoagulation: may be a reasonable alternative based on patient preference; the evidence for anticoagulation is more extensive. Class 2b

    Device-detected AF (pacemaker, ICD, loop recorder)

    Longest episodeAnticoagulate?
    ≥ 24 h, CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥ 2Reasonable, shared decision Class 2a

    Why duration matters

    Stroke risk from device-detected episodes is lower than from clinical AF and rises with episode length and with the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. Very short episodes are often artefact or brief atrial tachycardia and are not linked to clinical events. Confirm the tracing before acting on a device count.

    Also

    • Aspirin, alone or with clopidogrel, is not a substitute for anticoagulation. Class 3: Harm
    • Warfarin target INR 2.0 to 3.0 for AF. Class 1
    • After cardioversion or ablation of atrial flutter, anticoagulate at least 4 weeks. Class 1
    • Strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors and inducers change DOAC levels: check interactions. Class 1
